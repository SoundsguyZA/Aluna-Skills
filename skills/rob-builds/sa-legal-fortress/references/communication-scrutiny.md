# Communication Scrutiny Methodology

How to read an incoming legal or quasi-legal communication so that nothing is missed: not the explicit threat, not the implied threat, not the concession that was attempted, not the admission that was hidden, not the trap that was set for a careless reply.

This is the analytical engine of the skill. Apply it to every letter, email, WhatsApp message, board minute, or document Rob receives from any party in a contested matter.

## The Four Layer Read

Every communication operates on four layers simultaneously. Read each layer separately, then integrate.

**Layer 1: Surface text.** What does the document literally say. Quote the actual words. Do not paraphrase yet.

**Layer 2: Intended meaning.** What does the sender want the recipient to understand. This often differs from the literal text because lawyers, accountants, and family members in dispute use language designed to convey one meaning while preserving deniability about another.

**Layer 3: Strategic positioning.** What is the sender setting up for later. A statement made today becomes evidence tomorrow. A denial made today becomes a position they cannot retreat from. A concession made today narrows their options. Identify what the communication is positioning for.

**Layer 4: Omissions and avoidance.** What did the sender not say. What question did they not answer. What document did they not produce. What admission did they avoid. The silences in a legal communication are often more important than the words.

## Linguistic Markers and What They Mean

These patterns recur across SA legal correspondence. Each one signals something the sender wants you to miss.

**"Without prejudice".** Check whether the substance qualifies. If the letter contains a threat ("we will sue"), a demand ("pay us R50,000"), or a statement of position not aimed at settlement, the privilege may not attach and the letter may be admissible. Note when the heading is being used as a shield for something it should not cover.

**"Our client's position is..."** This signals a statement of intended posture, not a statement of fact. The lawyer is preserving wiggle room. The position can shift later without the attorney being personally compromised.

**"It appears that..." / "It seems that..." / "We understand that..."** Hedged factual assertions. The writer wants the reader to act on the assumption but is preserving deniability if the assumption proves wrong. Treat every such phrase as a non-admission.

**"In due course" / "At an appropriate time" / "In the fullness of time".** Timing evasion. The writer is refusing to commit to a deadline. This is almost always strategic delay.

**"We trust this matter can be resolved amicably."** Standard SA attorney boilerplate. Often immediately follows a threat. Reads as politeness, functions as an exit ramp for the writer to claim later that they were reasonable while their client behaved otherwise.

**"For the avoidance of doubt..."** Signals that the writer is locking in an interpretation they expect to be challenged. Read carefully: this is the writer's preferred construction and it will be the position they take in any subsequent proceedings.

**"Subject to the rights of our client which are reserved."** Boilerplate reservation. Usually meaningless except as evidence that the writer is preserving all options. Note its presence and move on.

**"Time is of the essence."** Strong legal language. Creates mora ex re. Treat any subsequent failure to perform on time as automatic breach.

**"You are hereby placed on terms."** Formal demand language. The writer is creating mora ex persona. The recipient now has a reasonable time to perform or be in breach.

**"We hold instructions to..."** The attorney is claiming a mandate from the client. This is not the same as the client's actual position. The attorney may have been over-instructed or may have softened the client's instructions.

**"Failing which..."** Conditional threat. Identify exactly what action will follow if the condition is not met. Vague threats ("we will take further steps") are often bluffs. Specific threats ("we will issue summons in the Durban High Court") are more credible.

**"Our client reserves the right to..."** Right reservation. Means nothing in itself unless coupled with a clear position. Frequently used to imply a threat without committing to one.

**Passive voice constructions.** "It has been brought to our attention that...", "Steps have been taken to...", "A decision was made to...". The writer is removing the human agent. Identify who actually did the thing. The actor is being hidden for a reason.

**Use of "you" vs use of name.** Switching between "Mr Barenbrug" and "you" within the same letter often signals an emotional tell. Where the writer is being formal, they use the name. Where they are being aggressive, they shift to "you". The shift point identifies the substantive grievance.

## Manipulation Patterns

These are not legal techniques. They are interpersonal tactics that show up in legal correspondence, particularly in family business disputes where personal and commercial relationships intersect.

**DARVO (Deny, Attack, Reverse Victim and Offender).** The party doing the harm denies the conduct, attacks the person raising it, and recasts themselves as the victim. Common pattern: "Rob's claims are baseless. His behaviour has been disruptive to the company. The family has been deeply hurt by his approach." Watch for the reversal.

**Gaslighting in writing.** Statements that contradict the recipient's known understanding of events, made with confident assertion. "As you know, no shareholders meeting was due in 2025." Where Rob knows otherwise, the statement is an attempt to make him doubt his own record. Counter by stating the contradicting fact with documentary support, calmly and once.

**Moving the goalposts.** A demand changes between communications. Last month the issue was that Rob was "uninvolved". This month it is that he is "interfering". Identify the inconsistency and confront it directly.

**Isolation tactics.** Suggestions that other family members or shareholders share a unified view contrary to Rob's. "The rest of the family agrees that..." This is rarely true and is designed to create a sense of isolation. Where Rob knows or suspects it is false, ask for confirmation from those allegedly aligned.

**Flooding.** Sending large volumes of correspondence, documents, or accusations to overwhelm. The strategic intent is to force errors or to create the appearance that Rob is uncooperative if he does not respond to every item. Counter by responding to material points only, in writing, with a clear record of what is being addressed and what is being held over.

**Selective disclosure.** Producing some documents while withholding others. The produced documents support the writer's narrative; the withheld documents would undermine it. Always ask explicitly for the documents not produced.

**False reasonableness.** Adopting a tone of reasonableness while the substance is unreasonable. The classic move: "We are willing to consider Rob's position but cannot agree to any of his demands until he withdraws his complaint and apologises for his conduct." Identify the unconditional surrender being requested under the appearance of reasonableness.

## Threat Identification

Categorise every threat by:

**Specificity.** Is the threat specific (which court, what cause of action, what remedy) or vague ("further action")?

**Credibility.** Does the writer have the means and will to execute the threat? An attorney letter threatening urgent High Court application is more credible than an email saying "we'll see you in court".

**Timeline.** Is there a deadline? Threats without deadlines are usually pressure tactics rather than imminent action.

**Cost.** What would the threat cost the writer to execute? Threats that would harm the writer financially or reputationally are less credible than threats that cost the writer little.

**Consistency.** Is the threat consistent with the writer's prior positions? A sudden new threat may indicate the writer has received new information or has shifted strategy.

For each threat, note whether to acknowledge, address, ignore, or counter. Not every threat must be answered. Sometimes silence is the best response. Sometimes a clear refutation is needed. Sometimes a counter-threat is appropriate. The choice depends on the strategic context.

## Hidden Admissions

Look for these patterns where the writer has accidentally conceded something:

**Use of past tense for disputed facts.** "When Rob withdrew from the operational role..." treats as fact what may be the central dispute. Capture this as an admission.

**Adjectives that imply causation.** "Following Rob's regrettable conduct..." accepts a sequence and characterises Rob's conduct adversely. The sequence may be admissible against the writer if it later helps Rob.

**References to documents not yet produced.** "As reflected in the management accounts..." admits the existence of management accounts. Demand production.

**Identification of decision makers.** "The board decided to..." admits a board decision. Demand the resolution. If no resolution exists, the decision is procedurally invalid.

**Statements about state of mind.** "Rob has indicated his unhappiness with..." admits that Rob's unhappiness was communicated and received. This neutralises any later argument that the dispute came out of nowhere.

## The Output

After running the four layer read and applying the markers and patterns, produce a structured output for Rob:

A quote-by-quote breakdown of the most significant phrases, with decoded meanings.

A list of threats, categorised by specificity, credibility, timeline, cost, and consistency.

A list of admissions, both express and accidental, that can be used against the sender.

A list of omissions and avoidance: what the sender did not say, what they did not produce, what they did not answer.

A strategic read: what is the sender trying to achieve, what is their likely next move, and what is the best response posture (silence, acknowledge, address, counter, escalate).

A recommended reply with the specific points to make, the specific points to omit, and the specific traps to avoid.
