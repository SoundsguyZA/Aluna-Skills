# SA Companies Act 71 of 2008 Reference

The workhorse statute for any shareholder, director, or company governance dispute in South Africa. Rob's Rib Rage matter lives almost entirely inside this Act, supplemented by the company's MOI and any shareholders agreement.

## Structural Hierarchy

When analysing any company dispute, the order of authority is:

The Companies Act 71 of 2008 and its Regulations.
The company's Memorandum of Incorporation (MOI), which replaced the old Memorandum and Articles of Association from 1 May 2011.
Any shareholders agreement, which under section 15(7) of the Act is void to the extent it conflicts with the Act or the MOI.
The common law, where the Act is silent.

Always ask: what does the MOI say. Many disputes are won or lost on a clause buried in the MOI that one party has forgotten about. Rib Rage's MOI must be obtained and read in full before any substantive position is taken.

## Directors' Duties (Sections 75 to 77)

**Section 75: Personal financial interests.** A director must disclose any personal financial interest in any matter to be considered by the board. Failure to disclose can void the resolution and trigger personal liability. In a family business like Rib Rage, this section is routinely breached and rarely enforced, which makes it a powerful lever.

**Section 76: Standards of directors' conduct.** Codifies the fiduciary duties:
- Duty to act in good faith and for a proper purpose (s76(3)(a))
- Duty to act in the best interests of the company (s76(3)(b))
- Duty of care, skill and diligence (s76(3)(c))

The "business judgement rule" in s76(4) protects a director who acted in good faith, with reasonable belief in the rationality of the decision, and after taking reasonably diligent steps to be informed. It does not protect bad faith, self-dealing, or wilful exclusion of co-shareholders from decisions they are entitled to participate in.

**Section 77: Liability of directors and prescribed officers.** A director can be held personally liable for any loss suffered by the company as a consequence of breaching sections 75 or 76, or for acting in the name of the company without authority, or for being party to reckless or fraudulent trading. Three-year prescription period from when the loss was incurred or the conduct discovered.

## Shareholder Remedies (The Heavy Weapons)

**Section 162: Delinquent director and probation orders.** A shareholder, director, company secretary, prescribed officer, or registered trade union can apply to court for a declaration that a director is delinquent or under probation. Grounds include gross negligence, wilful misconduct, breach of trust, or grossly abusing the position of director. Delinquency disqualifies a person from being a director for at least seven years.

**Section 163: Relief from oppressive or prejudicial conduct.** The most important section for any minority or excluded shareholder. A shareholder or director may apply to court for relief where:

- An act or omission of the company has had a result that is oppressive or unfairly prejudicial to, or unfairly disregards the interests of, the applicant; or
- The business of the company is being or has been carried on or conducted in a manner that is oppressive or unfairly prejudicial to, or unfairly disregards the interests of, the applicant; or
- The powers of a director or prescribed officer are being or have been exercised in a manner that is oppressive or unfairly prejudicial to, or unfairly disregards the interests of, the applicant.

Court can order almost anything to remedy the conduct: restraining the conduct, varying the MOI, requiring the company or other shareholders to buy out the applicant's shares at a fair price determined by the court, appointing directors, varying transactions, declaring agreements void, awarding damages, or winding up the company.

This is Rob's primary weapon. "Unfair disregard of interests" is a lower bar than oppression and is the practical standard most s163 applications succeed on. Conduct such as exclusion from board meetings, denial of access to financial records, payment of disproportionate remuneration to controlling family members, or refusal to pay legitimate dividends or consulting fees while the company is solvent and profitable, all fit within this section.

**Section 164: Dissenting shareholders appraisal rights.** Triggered by specific corporate actions: amendment of the MOI affecting the shareholder's rights, fundamental transaction such as a disposal of all or greater part of assets, merger or amalgamation, or scheme of arrangement. The dissenting shareholder can demand the company purchase their shares at fair value. Strict procedural requirements; miss a step and the right is gone.

**Section 165: Derivative action.** A shareholder, director, prescribed officer, or registered trade union can serve a demand on the company to commence or continue legal proceedings to protect the legal interests of the company. If the company refuses or fails to act within the prescribed period, the applicant can approach the court for leave to bring proceedings in the name of and on behalf of the company. Used where directors are looting the company and refuse to sue themselves.

## Shareholder Information Rights

**Section 26: Access to company records.** A shareholder has the right to inspect and copy, without charge, the company's MOI, register of members, minutes of shareholders meetings, annual financial statements, and notices and minutes of all members meetings. Refusal is an offence and grounds for a Companies Tribunal complaint or court application.

**Section 31: Access to financial information.** Annual financial statements must be available to every shareholder. Where statements have not been audited or independently reviewed, the shareholder may demand and receive a copy.

**Section 61: Shareholders meetings.** Annual general meeting must be held within 18 months of incorporation and thereafter once each calendar year, within 15 months of the previous AGM. Shareholders holding at least 10% of voting rights can demand a shareholders meeting be convened. Failure to convene is grounds for Companies Tribunal or court intervention.

## The Companies Tribunal

Established under section 193 of the Act. Faster and cheaper than the High Court for specific matters:

- Disputes about the company name
- Director disqualification or removal
- Reviews of certain CIPC decisions
- Some s163 type complaints can be routed here informally

Not a substitute for the High Court for substantive s163, s162, or s165 applications, but useful for early procedural moves and for cost effective pressure.

## Common Law Overlay

Where the Act is silent, common law principles continue to apply. The Foss v Harbottle "proper plaintiff" rule has been substantially codified into s165, but the common law underpinnings still inform interpretation. The fiduciary duty doctrine, the principle that directors must not place themselves in a position of conflict, and the no-profit rule, remain alive.

## Key Procedural Points

Most applications under sections 162, 163, and 165 are brought in the High Court by way of motion proceedings (notice of motion supported by founding affidavit). Pleadings are not used. Evidence is on affidavit. Cross-examination is rare.

Founding affidavit must set out:
- The applicant's standing (shareholder, director, etc.)
- The conduct complained of, with dates, documents, and witnesses
- The legal basis for the relief sought
- The specific relief sought (often pleaded in the alternative)

The respondent files an answering affidavit. The applicant may file a replying affidavit.

Time limits: there is no fixed prescription period under the Act for s163 applications, but delay can be raised as a defence (the Plascon-Evans rule applies on disputed facts, generally favouring the respondent on motion).

## Rib Rage Application

Apply this framework to the Rib Rage matter as follows:

The starting position is that Rob is a co-founder and shareholder. His shareholding percentage must be confirmed from the CIPC records (CoR 39 / share register) and the MOI obtained. Any shareholders agreement signed at founding or thereafter must be located.

If Rob has been excluded from board participation, denied access to financial records, denied legitimate consulting payments while controlling family members extract value, or had his shareholder rights effectively neutralised, the strongest claim is under section 163 for unfair disregard of his interests.

The consulting proposal at R35k/month with a 5-year evergreen term, framed as preserving shareholder rights, is the open record that establishes Rob attempted to negotiate before litigating. Its rejection or being ignored becomes Exhibit A in a s163 application showing the controlling shareholders refused a reasonable commercial accommodation.

The s163 remedy Rob would seek would likely be one or more of:
- An order that the company or the other shareholders purchase his shares at a fair value determined by an independent valuer
- An order regulating the future conduct of the company including board representation rights
- Damages for past unfairly prejudicial conduct
- In the alternative, winding up of the company on the just and equitable ground (section 81(1)(d)(iii) of the Act)

Section 81(1)(d)(iii) winding up is the nuclear option. It is sometimes pleaded as alternative relief in a s163 application to focus the controlling shareholders' minds on a buyout, since they generally prefer to buy out the minority than face liquidation of a profitable family business.
