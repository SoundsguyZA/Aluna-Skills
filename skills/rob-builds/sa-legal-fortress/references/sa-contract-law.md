# SA Contract Law Reference

South African contract law is Roman-Dutch common law with statutory overlays (CPA, NCA, etc.). It is not English law. Concepts like "consideration" do not exist. The core requirement is consensus (consensus ad idem) achieved through offer and acceptance, supported by capacity, lawfulness, possibility, and formalities where prescribed.

## Formation

**Offer:** A statement made with the intention that, upon acceptance, a contract will come into existence. Must be clear, complete, and made with serious intent (not jest or social engagement).

**Acceptance:** Must mirror the offer (mirror image rule). A purported acceptance that varies the terms is a counter-offer, which terminates the original offer.

**Consensus:** Both parties must agree on the same material terms. Where there is a material mistake (error in negotio, error in persona, error in corpore, error in substantia), there is no consensus and no contract, unless the doctrine of quasi-mutual assent (the Smith v Hughes principle as received into SA law) operates to bind the party who reasonably appeared to be agreeing.

**Capacity:** Parties must have legal capacity. Companies act through their authorised representatives. The Turquand rule protects third parties dealing with a company in good faith from internal procedural irregularities, but does not protect against actual knowledge of lack of authority.

**Lawfulness:** A contract whose performance is unlawful or contrary to public policy is void. Examples include contracts to commit a crime, contracts in restraint of marriage, and excessive restraints of trade.

**Possibility:** Initial impossibility renders a contract void. Supervening impossibility may discharge the contract under the doctrine of supervening impossibility of performance, provided the impossibility is not due to either party's fault.

**Formalities:** Most contracts can be oral. The Alienation of Land Act 68 of 1981 requires sale of immovable property to be in writing and signed. The General Law Amendment Act requires suretyships to be in writing. Electronic Communications and Transactions Act 25 of 2002 governs electronic signatures.

## Interpretation

The leading authority is Natal Joint Municipal Pension Fund v Endumeni Municipality 2012 (4) SA 593 (SCA). Interpretation is the objective process of attributing meaning to the words used in the contract, considered in light of:
- The language used
- The context (the contract as a whole)
- The purpose of the provision
- The factual matrix known to the parties at the time of contracting

This is the unitary approach. The old distinction between admitting extrinsic evidence to resolve ambiguity versus refusing it absent ambiguity is gone.

The parol evidence rule still excludes extrinsic evidence that contradicts, varies, or adds to the terms of a written contract intended to be the exclusive memorial of the agreement. But evidence of context and purpose is admissible to interpret the terms.

## Tacit Terms

A term not expressly included may be imported as a tacit term where it is necessary to give business efficacy to the contract (the officious bystander test as adapted in SA law). A tacit term must be:
- Necessary, not merely reasonable
- So obvious that the parties would have agreed to it if asked at the time
- Capable of clear and certain formulation

Implied terms (terms implied by law) operate differently and are imposed by statute, common law, or trade usage regardless of the parties' actual intention.

## Breach

Five forms of breach in SA law:

**Mora debitoris (debtor's delay):** The debtor fails to perform on the due date. Where time is of the essence (mora ex re), the debtor is in breach automatically. Otherwise (mora ex persona), the creditor must place the debtor in mora by demanding performance within a reasonable time.

**Mora creditoris (creditor's delay):** The creditor unjustifiably refuses or fails to accept proper performance.

**Positive malperformance:** The debtor performs but performance is defective or incomplete.

**Repudiation:** The debtor indicates by word or conduct, without lawful excuse, that they do not intend to perform their contractual obligations. The innocent party may elect to accept the repudiation (cancelling the contract) or reject it and insist on performance.

**Prevention of performance:** Either party renders performance impossible through their own conduct.

## Remedies for Breach

**Specific performance.** The primary remedy in SA law (different from English law where damages are primary). The court retains a discretion to refuse specific performance where it would be unjust, impossible, or against public policy, but the starting point is enforcement.

**Cancellation.** Available where the breach is material, or where the contract contains a lex commissoria (cancellation clause), or where the breach amounts to repudiation. The cancelling party must give notice of cancellation. After cancellation, restitution applies: each party restores what they received.

**Damages.** Aim to place the innocent party in the financial position they would have occupied had the contract been properly performed (positive interesse). The damages must:
- Flow directly from the breach (factual causation)
- Not be too remote (legal causation, the Victoria Falls test as developed)
- Be proved on a balance of probabilities

Mitigation duty applies. The plaintiff must take reasonable steps to mitigate loss.

**Interdict.** Available where the breach is ongoing or threatened and damages would not be an adequate remedy. The Setlogelo requirements apply: clear right, injury actually committed or reasonably apprehended, no other satisfactory remedy.

## Estoppel

Where a party makes a representation (by word or conduct), and the other party reasonably relies on that representation to their prejudice, the representor is estopped from denying the truth of the representation. Estoppel is a shield, not a sword: it prevents a party from going back on their representation but does not create new rights.

The requirements are:
- A representation
- Made by word or conduct
- Reasonably relied upon
- Prejudicially

## Without Prejudice Communications

Communications made bona fide in an attempt to settle a dispute are inadmissible as evidence of the matters they contain. The protection extends to without prejudice negotiations and offers.

The protection does not extend to:
- Statements that are not genuinely aimed at settlement
- Statements containing threats or admissions on collateral matters not subject to settlement
- The fact of negotiations themselves (admissible to explain delay)
- Statements made in the course of without prejudice negotiations that constitute new actionable wrongs (defamation, intimidation)

Marking a letter "without prejudice" does not magically confer the privilege if the substance does not qualify. Conversely, the privilege can apply to letters that omit the heading if the substance qualifies.

In SA practice, parties often mark letters "without prejudice save as to costs". This means the letter cannot be referred to on the merits but can be shown to the court after judgment when costs are being argued, typically as a Calderbank offer to demonstrate that the recipient should have settled and ought to bear costs.

## Rib Rage Application

The consulting proposal at R35k/month with 5-year evergreen term, sent to Mike Barenbrug with a 7-day response window, operates on several contract law levels:

It is an offer in the technical sense. If accepted, a binding consulting agreement comes into existence. The terms must therefore be clear and complete enough that acceptance creates an enforceable contract.

It is also a step in dispute escalation. Sent on the open record (not marked without prejudice), the offer and any response or non-response becomes admissible in subsequent proceedings. This is deliberate. The proposal becomes Exhibit A demonstrating that Rob attempted reasonable commercial accommodation and the controlling party refused or ignored him.

The 7-day response window creates a deadline for acceptance. After 7 days the offer lapses (unless extended) and Rob is free to escalate without any imputation that he acted precipitately.

The framing as "preserves shareholder rights" is critical. It signals that the consulting arrangement is in addition to, not in substitution for, Rob's shareholder entitlements. Mike cannot accept the consulting fee and later argue Rob compromised his shareholder claims by accepting it.

If Mike responds with a counter-offer (different terms), that is a rejection of the original offer and a new offer. Rob is free to accept, reject, or counter again.

If Mike ignores the proposal, the silence operates as constructive rejection. Silence is not acceptance in SA law (the qui tacet consentire principle is generally rejected in modern SA contract law).
